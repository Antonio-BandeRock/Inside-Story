**Real, confirmed race condition in `FoodLookup.tsx` fixed the same day**, found while testing the bell pepper fix: switching to a new food reset `prepMethod` to null synchronously, but the resolve effect's guard used the *previous* food's `prepMethods.length` (stale, since the new food's own prep-method list loads asynchronously) to decide whether it was safe to resolve yet. When the previous food had no prep choices to disambiguate, the resolve effect fired immediately for the new food with `prepMethod` still null, before the async "default to Raw" lookup (added 2026-08-01) had landed — `resolveFoodChoice()` then fell back to whichever row has no prep_method tag at all, which for foods like Sweet Pepper is a canned variant (confirmed: sweet peppers and carrots both did this). Fixed with an explicit `prepMethodsLoading` flag the resolve effect now waits on, instead of inferring readiness from list length.

**A real mid-build lesson from doing Side→Salad→Smoothie→Fermentation as mechanical copy+rename passes, worth remembering for the remaining six builders**: a plain word-boundary-safe find/replace (`\bSide\b`→`Salad`, `\bside\b`→`salad`) is NOT enough on its own — it reliably misses camelCase identifiers where the target word isn't at a real word boundary (`finishSide`, `setDishName`, `SideIngredient` mid-token), and it can also wrongly rewrite unrelated idioms that happen to contain the word as a substring (`side by side`, `near side` both got corrupted into nonsense on the Salad pass and had to be caught and reverted by hand). The Fermentation pass fixed this properly instead of catching it after the fact: idiom phrases (`side by side`, `near side`, `sideways`) were swapped for unique placeholder tokens *before* running the rename, then restored afterward — combined with a real verification grep sweep for the raw substring case-insensitively (not just the word-bounded pattern) after every pass, this caught everything cleanly on the first try. Worth reusing this exact two-step approach (protect-then-rename, then verify) for each remaining builder.

## App name: Inside Story

Chosen after a long naming process that ruled out, in order: Aurelia, Chrysalis, and Monarch (each had a direct existing product/trademark collision), Second Nature (an established NHS-recommended weight-loss app of the same name), Food Sense (multiple existing food/symptom-tracking apps, one an almost exact concept match), FoodFlex (multiple existing nutrition-tracking apps), and Backstory (an existing storytelling-magazine app, different category but same exact name). Understory and Body of Work also cleared the check but lost out to Inside Story on fit.

Inside Story is a real English idiom (the hidden truth behind what's visibly happening) rather than an invented word — chosen deliberately for warmth and natural phonetics over a mechanical acronym/blend construction, after early invented candidates (Nectiva, Discerna, etc.) tested as too clinical-sounding. Web search turned up no colliding app in the health/nutrition/food-tracking space under this name.

**ASO / discoverability plan**: the app name doesn't need to carry the search keywords itself — Play Store/App Store listings have a separate short-description/subtitle field for that. Precedent: "Paloma: Thyroid Hormone Health" ranks for thyroid-related searches on a distinctive brand name plus a keyword-rich subtitle. Plan: pair "Inside Story" with a subtitle along the lines of "Hashimoto's & Autoimmune Food Tracker" to cover real search terms (hashimoto's diet app, autoimmune food symptom tracker, thyroid food tracker, AIP diet app, food trigger tracker) without sacrificing the name itself.

**Name-protection checklist status:**
- Domain `insidestoryapp.com` — confirmed available by user via registrar check (2026-07-18). Not yet registered — should be registered promptly before someone else takes it. Recommended registrar: Namecheap or Porkbun (not GoDaddy — real multi-year breach history 2019-2022 plus a 2026 FTC settlement over security failings; user's own antivirus flagged the GoDaddy site directly).
- Domain variants checked: `insidestory.app` is taken (an unrelated book-discovery/ARC platform), `insidestory.com` is taken (an unrelated home goods/bedding company) — neither conflicts with the app itself. `insidestoryapp.net` appears likely available and is a reasonable cheap defensive registration alongside the .com. Skipping `.io` deliberately — unrelated to the brand thematically, and carries real (if currently unresolved) long-term uncertainty tied to the 2025 UK-to-Mauritius Chagos Islands sovereignty transfer that the `.io` ccTLD is legally tied to.
- Social handles (@insidestoryapp on Instagram/Twitter/TikTok) — no existing account found via search, not yet confirmed via each platform's own signup check.
- USPTO trademark search — done by user directly via uspto.gov/trademarks/search. Two Live "INSIDE STORY" wordmark registrations exist (Class 035, real estate marketing / FloorPlanOnline LLC; Class 009, bedding-industry marketing training tapes / BIA IP Holdco LLC) — both in unrelated goods/services categories, no likelihood-of-confusion conflict with a health/nutrition app. No Live registration found in Class 042 (software/SaaS) or Class 044 (health/medical/nutrition services), the two categories that would actually matter. Name is clear to proceed with informally; a real trademark attorney clearance search is still the recommended step before ever filing to register the mark itself.

## The core purpose (read this before making any scope trade-off)

This is not a generic nutrition tracker. The primary job: help someone with an autoimmune disease (Hashimoto's thyroiditis) relearn how and what to eat, and understand — personally, for their own body — how food affects them. Meal building, shopping lists, and scheduling are scaffolding that make daily use sustainable enough to generate that understanding; they are not the point on their own. When in doubt about priority, favor whatever helps someone discover and act on their own personal food-effect patterns over convenience features.

**Gut/microbiome healing is an explicit, named goal**, not just a side effect of good food choices — the app is also meant to actively help repair the person's microbiome and gut health, not just help them choose foods that avoid triggering symptoms.

**Refined framing (user's own words):** the real-world problem is that people are overwhelmed and confused about food, and most of them can't put the pieces together themselves the way a motivated, detail-oriented person might. The app's job is to remove that cognitive burden entirely — do the correlation/discovery work *for* the person, so day-to-day eating becomes a matter of following clear, pre-solved rules ("play by the rules of food, chemistry, etc.") rather than an ongoing research project. The complexity (matching foods to body chemistry, catching interactions, finding personal patterns) should live inside the app and its rules engine; what the person experiences should be ease — a grocery list that's already built for them, a plate that's already been figured out, so they can become the healthiest version of themselves without having to become their own nutritionist first. This reframes discovery/trend-finding as something the app does on the user's behalf, not a task handed to them.

## Architecture decisions made

*None of the cloud-backup, sync, encryption, or household-sharing architecture described in this section has been implemented yet — all app code so far is local-only, single-device (see Status above). This section remains the decided plan for when that work starts.*

**Local-first, no company server holding health data.** The live/authoritative database lives on the primary account holder's device. There is no backend server storing anyone's health data — full stop. This is a deliberate trust/privacy stance, not just a technical choice.

**Encrypted backup to the user's own cloud drive.** The primary device periodically writes an encrypted backup/snapshot to a folder in the user's own OneDrive/Google Drive/etc. (their account, their storage). This backup serves two purposes: disaster recovery, and (see below) transport for household sharing. The backup is not a readable file without importing it back into the app.

**Recovery model, chosen deliberately.** Encryption is protected by a master password or a locally-generated recovery key shown to the user once during setup (the same pattern password managers use). The app never holds the user's key — meaning there is intentionally no "forgot password" flow. This is the accepted cost of a genuine zero-knowledge design, not an oversight.

**Platform scope (revised): Android, iPhone, Windows, and Mac.** The app is no longer mobile-only — it needs to be usable from all four.

**Desktop technology — decided.** Expo already supports a "web" build target (via React Native Web), built on the same codebase as the mobile app — no separate toolchain needed. This web build is configured as an installable PWA (Progressive Web App): the UI and most business logic are shared with mobile; the local storage layer needs a platform-specific implementation (browser IndexedDB instead of on-device SQLite), and any native-only modules need web equivalents.

- **Windows**: full traditional "install from the Microsoft Store" experience, via PWABuilder packaging the PWA into an MSIX for Store submission. Zero platform fee — Microsoft eliminated individual developer registration fees in September 2025, and extended free registration to company accounts as well as of a Windows Developer Blog post in May 2026.
- **Mac**: installable PWA, not listed in the Mac App Store. This deliberately avoids Apple's Developer Program fee ($99/year, recurring, required for Mac App Store distribution regardless of framework) and its notarization/review process — a PWA installed via the browser's own "Add to Dock"/install mechanism never enters Apple's app-signing pipeline at all, unlike a traditionally distributed native binary (e.g., an Electron-wrapped app), which would still need notarization even outside the App Store to avoid Gatekeeper warnings. Revisit only if a future need for full Mac App Store presence outweighs the ongoing fee.

**Multi-device sync & control model (the person's own devices).** Refined from an earlier "one primary device is authoritative" framing into a cleaner, symmetric model: the encrypted cloud-backup folder (same location already described below) is the actual current state, not just a disaster-recovery copy. A device pulls the latest encrypted snapshot, decrypts it locally with the master password/key, and works with that as its own live local database from that point — preserving full offline capability, since the cloud only needs to be reachable at the pull/push transition points, not during everyday use (logging a meal with no signal still works). When done, the device pushes an updated encrypted snapshot back. This one mechanism now does three jobs: disaster-recovery backup, syncing between the same person's own phone/Windows/Mac, and the transport for household companion sharing described below (with the companion's access scope restricted, versus full access for the primary user's own devices).

Three layered safety mechanisms prevent two devices from editing the same data at once, fastest/most common to slowest/most rare:

1. *Locking ("control")*: whichever device pulls the database first holds exclusive control, recorded in the cloud metadata. A commit does not release control by itself — control persists until the app is closed, the screen times out/locks, or (if the person doesn't use a screen lock) an inactivity timer detects the app sitting open and idle in the foreground.
2. *Active takeover*: from a second device, the person taps a "take over/connect" button. This sends a *visible*, tap-required push notification to the controlling device — deliberately not a silent background push, since silent pushes are documented as best-effort and can be delayed or dropped by iOS/Android background throttling, while visible notifications are delivered far more reliably (that's the core job a notification system is built to do). The requesting device's screen names the controlling device by name (e.g., "Your iPhone currently has control") and instructs the person to check the notification there. Tapping that notification runs a background action that commits current local state and releases control; on iOS/Android the app then drops into a locked, read-only "this session moved to your other device" screen (apps can't force-quit themselves on mobile OSes the way a desktop program can; on Windows/Mac the app can cleanly exit itself, which is normal there). If the controlling device doesn't respond within roughly 1-2 minutes (a human needs to notice and tap, not an instant background process), the requesting device offers "take control anyway" — showing exactly how stale the other device's last save was (e.g., "iPhone last saved 2 hours ago") so the person makes an informed, explicit choice about what might be lost, rather than a silent automatic override.
3. *Stale-lock expiration*: a hard maximum lease of 30 minutes on control, independent of the graceful signals above — if no renewal happens in that window (crashed app, dead battery, lost/stolen device, permanent connectivity loss), control auto-expires regardless, so a single unreachable device can never permanently lock the person out of their own data.

Underneath all three, last-write-wins plus a visible activity log remains as the final safety net for whatever narrow edge cases slip past the above (e.g., cloud-sync propagation delay letting two devices briefly both believe they hold control) — the same conflict policy already used for household companion sync below, reused rather than inventing a second system. This design deliberately reuses two pieces already decided elsewhere in this document: the minimal content-blind push relay (APNs/FCM) originally built for reminders' cross-device nudges is the same signal used for takeover notifications, and the "show your own freshness/confidence" principle originally built for reminders is reused for the "last saved X ago" risk disclosure during a forced takeover.

**Household sharing, two access tiers, both reading from a shared cloud folder:**
- *Free viewer companion*: the primary shares their cloud backup folder (via the cloud provider's own native folder-sharing, not a custom mechanism) to a second person. That person's app reads periodic snapshots from the shared folder — modeled on how Anki's "shared decks" feature works (one-way, periodic reimport that merges in new content without wiping the viewer's own local state; no live subscription/auto-update exists in Anki either, and that absence is fine here too). The viewer can see the meal schedule, shopping list, and trend reports, and query details — but cannot edit most things.
- *One narrow exception*: the viewer CAN check off shopping list items. This is treated as a distinct, safe category of "write-back" — idempotent, single-field, low-conflict-risk actions (checking off a list item, marking a scheduled task complete) — sent back to the primary as a small delta/answer file. This is different in kind from full write access, and deliberately so: no general merge logic is needed for this category.
- *Paid interactive companion*: real read-write access to a shared "household domain" (meal plan, shopping list, schedule) — but NOT to the primary's "personal health domain" (symptom logs, labs, supplements, their own scoring/trend history), which stays owned by the primary and unreachable by any companion account, even the paid one. This split exists for both security and product-trust reasons.
- *Conflict handling for the interactive tier*: last-write-wins per field, plus a visible activity log so nothing silently disappears ("Alex removed milk at 4:12pm, overriding your edit from 4:10pm"). This is intentionally not a heavyweight CRDT/merge system — validated by the fact that AnkiWeb's own mature, decades-refined sync still falls back to a full one-way overwrite when changes genuinely can't merge (e.g., structural changes). Revisit only if real usage shows last-write-wins isn't good enough.
- *Cross-provider question, unresolved*: does household sharing require both people to use the same cloud provider, or must it support inviting someone on a different provider? Needs a decision before onboarding UX is designed.

**Reminders/scheduling, contextual from day one (not clock-based).** A reminder like "take your levothyroxine" should account for what's actually been logged — e.g., warn that a calcium-rich food logged earlier pushes the safe window later — not just fire at a fixed preset time. This splits into two different mechanisms:
- Fully local, on-device scheduled notifications (no server) for anything self-contained and recomputed instantly whenever the user is actively using the app.
- A minimal, content-blind push relay (APNs/FCM) only for cross-device nudges ("the other person just changed something") — this is a real server component, but it never carries actual health content, only a wake-up signal.
- Real mobile OS constraint: background execution is throttled on both iOS and Android, so "recompute the moment something changes" is only guaranteed while the app is in the foreground; background recomputation is best-effort on the OS's own refresh schedule, not instant. Design around this rather than assuming perfect real-time background updates.
- Reminders should show their own confidence/freshness (e.g., "based on food logged as of 7:45am") so the user can tell when contextual guidance might be based on stale data — because a late-logged contextual reminder can be confidently wrong, which is arguably worse than a dumb static reminder being honestly uninformed.

**Interaction rules engine — two kinds, both available from day one, kept visually distinct:**
- *Built-in, cited rules*: prescription/supplement/food timing interactions (e.g., levothyroxine + calcium/iron absorption interference, biotin interfering with certain thyroid lab assays) — authored with the same real-citation discipline as the nutrition database (FDA labeling, endocrinology guidance, pharmacology literature), not assumed/common-knowledge rules. Distinct research effort from the existing D1-D6 food scoring. Should probably ship as a maintainable, versioned data file/ruleset that can update independently of app releases, similar to how antivirus/spam-filter rule updates work.
- *Personal rule builder*: lets the individual user encode their own discovered pattern or their own doctor's specific instruction to them (which might differ from the general guideline). Must be labeled distinctly from the cited rules so personal hypothesis is never confused with verified medical fact, including in any doctor-facing report.
- *Synergy to build toward*: the trend engine should be able to surface candidate personal rules from observed patterns ("you've logged fatigue within a day of high-goitrogen meals four times this month — want to turn that into a reminder?"), closing the loop between passive tracking and actionable personal knowledge.

**Late/retroactive logging**: must be allowed and low-friction — guided quick-select questions rather than a blank form (what did you eat/take → quick-select from favorites/recents → when did it actually happen → smart time picker). Every late entry should immediately show its consequence (recalculated schedule impact), turning logging into a small teaching moment rather than a chore. Scheduled reminders should double as catch-up checkpoints ("anything since we last talked?") rather than relying on the user to self-initiate a late entry.

## Monetization — tiers

- **Free**: single meal builder, general nutritional info only. No Hashimoto's 6-dimension scoring, no reporting, no trends.
- **First paid tier**: meal builder + shopping list, trends over time, the full Hashimoto's 6-dimension scoring, the daily meal/hydration/supplement/prescription schedule with contextual reminders, doctor/nutritionist/trainer reporting, household sharing (both companion tiers), and cloud backup.
- **Addon tiers, later**: sleep tracking, exercise tracking, lab result tracking/trending, and other future health-tracking features layer on as paid addons.
- **Household economics decision**: the paid interactive companion is a separate, presumably cheaper "companion/viewer-plus" seat rather than requiring a second full-price primary subscription — full-price-per-person was identified as a real conversion risk for a spouse who doesn't personally have Hashimoto's.

## Reporting

Doctor/nutritionist/trainer/self reports are different views over the same underlying time-stamped, taggable log data — designed so every log entry is timestamped and taggable from the start, since reporting becomes a query rather than a rebuild if the schema supports it from day one. Reports generate entirely on-device (PDF export, no server rendering) — consistent with the stated privacy model that the only data ever leaving the device is something the user explicitly exports/prints/emails themselves.

## Technology stack — decided

- **Framework: React Native, built on Expo.** Chosen over Flutter specifically because this project needs heavy third-party integration (OneDrive, Google Drive, push notifications, app store billing, encrypted local storage), and React Native's package ecosystem has broader off-the-shelf coverage for that kind of work — meaningful for a solo builder.
- **Why Expo specifically**: the user's dev hardware is a Windows PC + Android phone, no Mac (daughter has an iPhone, available for occasional testing only). Expo Go lets the user run the app live on their own Android phone during development with no Mac and no native build step. When a real installable build is needed later (including iOS), Expo's cloud build service (EAS Build) compiles it on Expo's servers — a Mac is never required, even for shipping to the App Store.
- **Builder's background**: 30 years as an IT Manager, "some scripting/basics" hands-on coding experience, no prior mobile app development, but a strong track record of learning new technical domains as needed. Building this themselves with ongoing step-by-step direction and code-level help from Claude Code in VS Code, not handing it to a separate developer.
- **First milestone: complete.** The "Hello World" toolchain validation (Node.js, VS Code, Expo tooling, Expo Go on the Android phone) succeeded and was superseded by real feature development starting 2026-07-19 — see Status above.

## Named, unresolved risks (product risk, not engineering risk)

1. **Logging discipline / retention**: the single biggest risk to the whole vision is whether a real person will log consistently enough, over weeks/months, for contextual reminders and trend-correlation to mean anything. Most health-tracking apps fail on this, not on missing features. Worth testing this specific assumption cheaply before building the full data model around it.
2. **Signal quality**: whether symptom/food correlation-finding on noisy, confounded, small-sample (n=1) personal data actually surfaces real patterns versus things that merely look like patterns. Needs to be handled honestly in the product (showing confidence, not asserting causation) rather than assumed to just work.

## Healing-journey stages — decided 2026-07-31, not yet built

The app will model where someone is in their Hashimoto's recovery and use that to drive guidance in the Food builders. Decisions made, implementation not started:

- **Framework**: the 5-stage *healing/regression* model (distinct from disease-progression staging), sourced from functional-medicine/root-cause thyroid protocols, associated with Dr. Izabella Wentz: **1. Triage** (symptom relief, hormone stabilization) → **2. Digging** (removing triggers, notably gluten/dairy) → **3. Gut Repair** (intestinal permeability, 4R protocol) → **4. Rebalancing** (immune modulation, HPA/adrenal) → **5. Maintenance** (clinical remission, antibodies in range).
- **Stage source**: self-declared during app setup (a new user has no assessment history), then adjusted over time by the existing Assessment. The app *suggests* a stage change rather than silently reclassifying.
- **What stage does**: **advisory + reordering only, never gating.** Stage-appropriate foods surface first in the pickers; anything questionable for the current stage gets a color-coded flag linked to an explanation. Nothing is ever hidden or blocked.
- **Framing**: presented as a *named, attributed framework*, with individual guidance items tagged by evidence strength — reusing the same discipline already in `lib/sixDimensionsReference.ts` (where AIP's nightshade exclusion is scored "Mild Risk" precisely because its evidence is mechanistic/elimination-based rather than RCT). This matters because evidence strength genuinely varies across the five stages: selenium lowering TPO antibodies has real randomized-trial support, while "adrenal fatigue"/HPA-reset framing and "leaky gut" as a clinical entity are contested in mainstream endocrinology. Functional-medicine root-cause staging is **not** mainstream endocrinology consensus, and the app must not imply otherwise.

**Sources supplied 2026-07-31** (the earlier blocker — bare [1]–[21] markers with no source list — is resolved). Listed here with an honest evidence tier per source, since they differ enormously and the app has committed to per-claim tiering:

| Claim | Source | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|
| Selenium reduces TPO antibodies over 3–6 months | Systematic meta-analysis of 21 RCTs, "Clinical Efficacy of Selenium Supplementation in Patients with Hashimoto Thyroiditis" (PubMed) | **Strong.** Meta-analysis of randomized trials, the highest tier in this set. |
| High-dose thiamine (B1) resolves autoimmune-thyroid fatigue | "Thiamine and Hashimoto's Thyroiditis: A Report of Three Cases", PubMed 24351023 | **Weak.** This is a 3-patient *case report*, not a trial — no control group, no randomization. The supplied text described it as "a landmark peer-reviewed clinical pilot trial", which overstates it. Must not be presented as trial evidence. |
| Gluten/dairy/soy as top inflammatory triggers | Patient-survey metrics, thyroidpharmacist.com | **Weak/indicative.** Self-reported survey of one practitioner's own audience: real signal, but selection-biased and uncontrolled. (Note the separate, stronger celiac/thyroid autoimmunity literature exists independently and is the better citation for the gluten claim specifically.) |
| Disease progression Stages 0–5; gland regeneration | Dr. Izabella Wentz, thyroidpharmacist.com articles | **Practitioner framework.** The framework's own author explaining it. Legitimate to attribute, not independent peer review. |
| Healing/regression staging; 90-day sequencing | Functional Medicine University clinical protocol guide; *Hashimoto's Protocol* (book) | **Practitioner framework.** Training-organization material and a trade book, not peer-reviewed literature. |
| Definition of clinical remission | Paloma Health patient-education page | **Secondary/patient-education.** A telehealth company's explainer, useful for plain-language framing, not a primary source. |

Net: exactly one claim in this set (selenium) carries strong trial-level evidence. Everything else is practitioner framework, case-report, or survey data. That is fine to build on **provided the app labels it honestly**, which is the whole point of the per-claim tiering decision above. Individual source URLs/claims still need verifying against the actual documents at build time rather than trusted from the summary.

**Already exists and should be reused, not rebuilt**: `app/assessment.tsx` (3 cited domains, 23 items, built for periodic retaking — this is the "self-assess at intervals" machinery) and `app/purple-digest.tsx` (placeholder shell; the intended home for this educational content, sources already chosen: MedlinePlus, ATA, Autoimmune Association). Nothing resembling a journey stage exists in the codebase yet — confirmed by search.

**Practical scoping note**: only stages 2 and 3 meaningfully drive *food* decisions. Stages 1, 4, and 5 are largely about hormone dosing, nutrient correction, adaptogens, sleep, and lab markers. The five stages are right for education and orientation, but builder rules will key off fewer distinctions than five.
